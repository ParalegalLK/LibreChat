# Writ Jurisdiction in Sri Lanka

---

## 📋 Introductory Note

### Nature and Concept of Writ Jurisdiction

A **writ** is a formal written command or order issued by a superior court, directing an inferior court, tribunal, public authority, or individual to do or refrain from doing a particular act. Writs are rooted in the prerogative powers of the courts and represent one of the most powerful instruments of **judicial review** available in Sri Lankan public law. \ue200\ue202turn2search2\ue202turn1search3\ue201

The writ jurisdiction is, broadly speaking, \ue203invoked to control the power of bodies which discharge duties of a public nature,\ue204\ue202turn1search3 ensuring that public authorities act lawfully, within their conferred powers, in conformity with the rules of natural justice, and consistent with mandatory legal provisions.

---

### Constitutional and Statutory Basis

The writ jurisdiction in Sri Lanka is **constitutionally entrenched** and operates primarily through two provisions of the Constitution of the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka:

| Constitutional Provision | Scope |
|---|---|
| **Article 140** | Confers power on the Court of Appeal to issue mandates in the nature of writs of **certiorari, prohibition, procedendo, mandamus, and quo warranto** |
| **Article 141** | Confers power on the Court of Appeal to issue **writs of habeas corpus** |

\ue200\ue202turn3search6\ue202turn3search5\ue201

**Article 140** provides that, subject to the provisions of the Constitution, \ue203the Court of Appeal shall have full power and authority to inspect and examine the records of any Court of First Instance or other institution and to grant and issue, according to law, orders in the nature of writs of certiorari, prohibition, procedendo, mandamus, and quo warranto.\ue204\ue202turn1search1

**Habeas corpus** is separately governed by **Article 141**, which is identified as a constitutional provision specifically intended to **safeguard the liberties of citizens.**\ue202turn8search6

An important feature of the Sri Lankan constitutional architecture is found in **Article 126(3)**, which provides that where, in the course of hearing a writ application, the Court of Appeal finds prima facie evidence of an **infringement or imminent infringement of a Fundamental Right** (Chapter III) or language right (Chapter IV), the Court of Appeal shall forthwith **refer the matter to the Supreme Court** for determination.\ue202turn3search3

> 🔑 The writ jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal is **exclusively governed by Article 140 of the Constitution** and cannot be ousted by ordinary legislation. As held in *B. Sirisena Cooray v. Tissa Dias Bandaranayake* [1999] 1 SLR 1, the writ jurisdiction of the Superior Courts is conferred by the Constitution, which is the supreme law of the country, and therefore ordinary statutory ouster clauses cannot strip the Court of Appeal of this jurisdiction.\ue202turn1search1

---

### General Principles Governing Writ Applications

Several threshold and general principles govern writ applications in Sri Lanka:

- **Subject Matter — Public Nature:** Writ jurisdiction is directed at bodies and persons discharging **public functions or duties**. Private acts of private individuals generally fall outside its scope.\ue202turn1search3
- **Locus Standi:** The person applying for an order in the nature of a writ must be a **person entitled to do so** — i.e., a person with sufficient legal interest in the subject matter of the application.\ue202turn5search2
- **Discretionary Remedy:** Writs (except habeas corpus) are, in the main, **discretionary** remedies. The Court will consider the conduct of the applicant, the availability of alternative remedies, and whether the grant of the writ would serve the interests of justice.\ue200\ue202turn2search2\ue202turn5search0\ue201
- **Habeas Corpus — Writ of Right:** By contrast, the writ of habeas corpus is not discretionary but a **writ of right** (*ex debito justitiae*) — it issues as of right once the applicant satisfies the court that the detention is unlawful.\ue202turn8search0
- **Supreme Court Writ Jurisdiction:** By the Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution, in relation to matters falling within the exercise of jurisdiction by a **Provincial High Court**, the writ jurisdiction formerly vested in the Court of Appeal under Article 140 is now vested in the **Supreme Court**.\ue202turn1search5

## ⚖️ Types of Writs and Grounds for Grant

---

### 1. 📜 Writ of Certiorari

**Purpose:** A writ of certiorari is issued to **quash or set aside** a decision, order, or determination already made by an inferior court, tribunal, or public authority that was made unlawfully.\ue200\ue202turn1search0\ue202turn2search2\ue201

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Excess or Lack of Jurisdiction** | The decision-maker acted beyond the powers conferred upon it by law, or entirely lacked the jurisdiction to act in the matter. |
| **Error of Law on the Face of the Record** | A clear error of law is apparent on the face of the record of the proceedings — the decision reflects a misapplication or misinterpretation of the law, discernible without going beyond the record itself. |
| **Breach of Natural Justice** | The decision was made in violation of the fundamental rules of natural justice: **(a) audi alteram partem** — the right to be heard; and **(b) nemo judex in causa sua** — the rule against bias or conflict of interest. |
| **Fraud or Mala Fides** | The decision was obtained by fraud or was made in bad faith. |

\ue200\ue202turn7search0\ue202turn4search1\ue202turn1search0\ue201

> **Important distinction:** Certiorari operates **retrospectively** — it corrects a decision already made. It is available where proceedings have been concluded or a decision has already been issued.

---

### 2. 🚫 Writ of Prohibition

**Purpose:** A writ of prohibition is issued to **prevent** an inferior court, tribunal, or public authority from **continuing to exercise** a jurisdiction that it does not lawfully possess, or from acting in violation of law. Unlike certiorari, it is **prospective** in operation — it halts ongoing proceedings before they are completed.\ue200\ue202turn2search2\ue202turn1search3\ue201

As stated by Sunil Cooray in *Principles of Administrative Law in Sri Lanka*, \ue203the Writ of Prohibition is available to prevent a proceeding in a given matter, to exercise a power which it does not have under the law, or act in violation of the rules of natural justice where the law requires such officer...\ue204\ue202turn2search0

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Excess or Lack of Jurisdiction** | The tribunal or authority is about to proceed in a matter over which it has no jurisdiction, or is about to exceed the limits of its jurisdiction. |
| **Breach of Natural Justice** | The tribunal or authority is proceeding in a manner that violates the rules of natural justice (right to be heard; rule against bias). |
| **Acting Contrary to Law** | The authority proposes to exercise a power not conferred by law or in a manner contrary to mandatory legal requirements. |

\ue200\ue202turn2search0\ue202turn4search0\ue201

> **Key distinction from certiorari:** Prohibition is directed at **future or ongoing** conduct; certiorari addresses **past** or already concluded decisions. Both can, and often are, sought together in the same application.\ue202turn0search2

---

### 3. 📣 Writ of Mandamus

**Purpose:** A writ of mandamus is issued to **compel** a public authority, body, officer, or inferior court to **perform a public duty** that it has failed or refused to discharge.\ue202turn2search2

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Existence of a Public Legal Duty** | There must be a clear legal duty imposed on the respondent by statute or public law — not merely a private contractual obligation. |
| **Public Character of the Duty** | The duty must be of a public nature, imposed in the public interest. |
| **Demand and Refusal** | The applicant must have made a demand upon the authority to perform its duty, and that demand must have been refused or ignored. |
| **Legal Right of Applicant** | The applicant must have a sufficient legal right to demand the performance of the duty in question. |
| **No Other Adequate Remedy** | Mandamus generally will not issue where there is another equally adequate remedy available. |

\ue200\ue202turn2search2\ue202turn4search0\ue202turn5search0\ue201

---

### 4. 🏛️ Writ of Quo Warranto

**Purpose:** A writ of quo warranto is applied for to have the court declare that a person who is **in de facto possession of a public office** is not *de jure* entitled to hold that office, thereby preventing the usurpation of a public office.\ue202turn2search2

As Blackstone described it, quo warranto is \ue203a writ of right for the king, against him who claims or usurps any office, franchise or liberty.\ue204\ue202turn2search3

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Existence of a Substantive Public Office** | The position in question must be a substantive public office created by law — not mere private employment or contract. |
| **Usurpation or Unlawful Holding** | The respondent must have unlawfully claimed, usurped, or be continuing to hold the office without lawful authority or entitlement. |
| **Disqualification in Law** | The respondent may be disqualified by law (e.g., statutory or constitutional disqualification) from holding the office in question. |

\ue200\ue202turn2search2\ue202turn2search3\ue202turn5search0\ue201

> **Note:** Quo warranto may be applied for **in combination with mandamus or certiorari** in appropriate cases.\ue202turn5search3

---

### 5. ⏩ Writ of Procedendo

**Purpose:** A writ of procedendo is issued to direct an inferior court or tribunal to **proceed with a matter** over which it has jurisdiction but has **wrongfully declined** to exercise it, or has unduly delayed the proceedings.\ue202turn1search1

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Wrongful Refusal of Jurisdiction** | The inferior court or tribunal has jurisdiction to hear the matter but has wrongfully declined or refused to exercise it. |
| **Undue Delay** | The inferior court has delayed proceedings in a manner that amounts to a denial of justice. |
| **Existence of Jurisdiction** | It must be established that the inferior court or tribunal does in fact have the legal jurisdiction to hear the matter. |

\ue200\ue202turn1search1\ue202turn2search6\ue201

---

### 6. 🔓 Writ of Habeas Corpus

**Purpose:** A writ of habeas corpus (Latin: *"you shall have the body"*) is issued to challenge the **lawfulness of the detention or imprisonment** of a person. It commands the custodian to produce the detained person before the court, and the court then examines whether the detention is lawful. If unlawful, the court orders the person's **immediate release.**\ue200\ue202turn8search0\ue202turn8search1\ue201

The writ of habeas corpus is governed by **Article 141** of the Constitution — separately from the other writs under Article 140 — and is classified as a **writ of right** (*ex debito justitiae*), not a discretionary remedy.\ue202turn8search0

#### Grounds for Grant:

| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Unlawful or Illegal Detention** | The foundational ground: the detention or imprisonment is not authorized by law, lacks legal justification, or violates the procedure established by law (see also Article 13 of the Constitution — liberty of person). |
| **Absence of Legal Authority** | The respondent detaining the person has no lawful authority to do so (e.g., detention without warrant, beyond permitted period, or without legal basis). |
| **Violation of Constitutional Rights** | The detention violates the fundamental rights of the detainee, including the right to personal liberty guaranteed by Article 13 of the Constitution. |
| **Procedural Illegality** | The procedure by which the person was arrested or detained was not in conformity with the law (e.g., failure to inform of grounds of arrest, failure to produce before a Magistrate within the stipulated period). |
| **Unlawful Continuation of Detention** | Even if the initial detention was lawful, the continued detention has become unlawful (e.g., expiry of a remand order, sentence fully served). |

\ue200\ue202turn8search0\ue202turn8search1\ue202turn8search3\ue202turn8search6\ue201

> **Procedural note:** The writ is only **formally issued at the conclusion** of the proceedings if the detention is found to be unlawful; the modern practice is not to require the production of the prisoner at the outset unless there are special circumstances, but to order release if imprisonment is found to be unlawful.\ue202turn8search0

# Writ Applications and the Availability of an Alternative Remedy — Sri Lanka

---

## Overview

One of the most commonly raised **preliminary objections** in writ applications before the Court of Appeal is that the petitioner had an alternative remedy available which was not pursued prior to invoking the court's writ jurisdiction. This objection goes to the **discretionary character** of writ relief and has been the subject of considerable judicial consideration in Sri Lanka, yielding a body of nuanced case law that balances the extraordinary nature of the writ remedy against the imperative of doing justice.

---

## The General Rule: Writ Jurisdiction as a Remedy of Last Resort

It is well settled in Sri Lankan law that \ue203when an alternative and equally efficacious remedy is available to a party, the party should be required to pursue that remedy before invoking the writ jurisdiction.\ue204\ue202turn6search0

This principle was crystallised in the seminal case of **University Council of Vidyodaya University v. Linus Silva** (66 NLR 505), which has been consistently cited by courts as the locus classicus for the proposition that a writ will ordinarily not lie where an alternative remedy is available.\ue200\ue202turn3search6\ue202turn3search3\ue201

The rationale for this rule was articulated clearly by Shirani Thilakawardena J in **Ishak v. Lakshman Perera, Director of Customs and Others** (2003) 3 SLR 18, where Her Ladyship held:

> *"Where there is an alternative procedure which will provide the applicant with a satisfactory remedy the Courts will usually insist on an applicant exhausting that remedy before seeking judicial review. In doing so the Court is coming to a discretionary decision ... For the Court to require the alternative procedure to be exhausted prior to resorting to judicial review is in accord with the judicial review being properly regarded as being a **remedy of last resort**. It is important that the process should not be clogged with unnecessary cases, which are perfectly capable of being dealt with in another tribunal."*\ue202turn6search0

The courts have also noted a policy dimension to this principle: where Parliament has by statute established an alternative procedure, \ue203it can also be the situation that Parliament, by establishing an alternative procedure, indicated either expressly or by implication that it intends that procedure to be used.\ue204\ue202turn6search0

In Sri Lankan law, both the writ remedy and the appellate remedy are **statute-based**. In this context, it is appropriate to characterise the appellate jurisdiction as the **ordinary jurisdiction** and review by way of writs of certiorari, prohibition and mandamus as the **extraordinary jurisdiction**. A party dissatisfied with a judgment or order where a right of appeal is given either directly or with leave must invoke and pursue the appellate jurisdiction.\ue202turn0search0

---

## The Threshold Requirement: What Must a Petitioner Do?

Following **SC Appeal No. 52/2021** (decided July 2023), the Supreme Court affirmed that \ue203it is necessary for the Petitioners in a writ application either to aver that the party had exhausted the alternative remedy, or explain why the party decided to invoke writ jurisdiction without resorting to the alternative remedy available to them.\ue204 Silence on this point is treated adversely by the Court.\ue202turn6search0

Accordingly, a writ petitioner who has not exhausted an available alternative remedy must, at minimum, **plead and explain the basis** upon which they chose to bypass that remedy and seek the extraordinary intervention of the Court.

---

## The Critical Qualification: The Alternative Remedy Must Be "Adequate"

Critically, however, this rule does not operate as an absolute bar. Both statute and case law make clear that the alternative remedy must be **adequate, satisfactory, speedy, and effective**. As the Supreme Court in *Somasunderam Vanniasingham v. Forbes and Another* [1993] 2 SLR 362 held, the critical question is \ue203whether the statutory remedy provides a sufficient satisfactory alternative to the discretionary remedy by way of writ.\ue204\ue202turn0search1

The same court considered the position summarised by S.M. Mehta in *Indian Constitutional Law* (1990 Edition):

> *"The existence of an alternative remedy may be a ground for refusing a writ of certiorari, where the defect of jurisdiction is not patent on the face of the record and fundamental rights are not involved. **This is a rule of convenience and not a rule of law** and hence certiorari may be issued even when an alternative remedy is available. Thus an alternative remedy that is not speedy, effective or adequate is no ground for refusing a writ of certiorari."*\ue202turn10search4

Furthermore, **Halsbury's Laws of England** (4th Edition, Vol 11, para 1528), cited with approval by the Supreme Court in *Somasunderam Vanniasingham*, states: \ue203There is no rule in certiorari as there is in mandamus, that it will lie only where there is no other equally effective remedy, and provided the requisite grounds exist, certiorari will lie although a right of appeal has been conferred by statute.\ue204\ue202turn10search4

Similarly, the court observed that \ue203delay in securing an effective other remedy has been considered unsatisfactory. The mere existence of some power to resist the binding effect of an unacceptable settlement order should not of itself be hastily regarded as a satisfactory alternative remedy to the Court's discretionary powers of review.\ue204\ue202turn0search1

The Court of Appeal has more recently confirmed (in **CA/WRT/582/2025**) that the existence of an adequate alternative remedy is \ue203not a mere technical bar, but a substantive consideration\ue204 — reflecting a contextual, fact-sensitive approach rather than a mechanical rule.\ue202turn1search2

---

## Key Principles Distilled from the Case Law

The following principles can be distilled from the Sri Lankan case law on this question:

| Principle | Effect |
|---|---|
| **General Rule** | Where an alternative and equally efficacious remedy exists, writ jurisdiction will ordinarily not be exercised. |
| **Duty to Plead** | A petitioner who bypasses an alternative remedy must aver this fact and explain the reason in the petition itself. |
| **Adequacy Test** | The alternative remedy must be *speedy, effective, adequate, and satisfactory*. If it is not, the Court retains its discretion to entertain the writ application. |
| **Rule of Convenience** | For certiorari in particular, the rule is one of *convenience*, not of law — the court retains discretion even where an alternative remedy exists. |
| **Parliament's Intent** | Where Parliament has created a specific appellate or review mechanism, the court will give weight to the legislative intent that it be used. |
| **Writ as Last Resort** | The writ jurisdiction is an extraordinary jurisdiction and should not be used to supplant or circumvent ordinary statutory appeals and review processes. |
| **Exceptional Circumstances** | Where there are truly exceptional circumstances — such as a patent and fundamental excess of jurisdiction, a breach of natural justice, or the involvement of constitutional or fundamental rights — the court may exercise its writ jurisdiction even where an alternative remedy is nominally available. |

\ue200\ue202turn6search0\ue202turn0search0\ue202turn0search1\ue202turn10search4\ue202turn1search2\ue201

---

## Exceptions: When a Writ May Lie Despite an Alternative Remedy

Courts in Sri Lanka have recognised, expressly or by implication, that writ jurisdiction may be exercised despite the existence of an alternative remedy in the following circumstances:

1. **Patent or manifest illegality / excess of jurisdiction** — where the defect in the impugned decision is so fundamental and apparent on the face of the record that the alternative remedy cannot adequately address it.\ue202turn10search4

2. **Breach of natural justice** — where the applicant's fundamental right to be heard or the rule against bias has been violated, courts have shown willingness to intervene by writ regardless of an available appeal.\ue202turn0search1

3. **Involvement of fundamental or constitutional rights** — where the violation complained of engages fundamental rights under Chapter III of the Constitution, the court will not necessarily confine the applicant to the alternative remedy.\ue202turn10search4

4. **Inadequacy, delay, or ineffectiveness of the alternative remedy** — including where the alternative remedy is cumbersome, is unlikely to provide timely relief, or cannot practically address the harm caused.\ue202turn0search1

5. **Fraud or bad faith** — where the impugned decision was procured by or tainted with fraud or mala fides, reliance on an internal appeal mechanism may be inappropriate.\ue202turn10search4

---

## Conclusion

The treatment of alternative remedies in Sri Lankan writ jurisprudence reflects a carefully calibrated balance. The writ jurisdiction, being extraordinary, is not to be lightly exercised where Parliament has provided an adequate alternative avenue of redress. Courts will, as a general matter, require a petitioner to have exhausted that avenue or to have provided a reasoned justification for bypassing it. However, this principle operates as a **rule of judicial discretion rather than a rigid rule of law**, and the courts retain their full authority to intervene by writ where the alternative remedy is not truly adequate or where the justice of the case demands it. The adequacy, speed, and efficacy of the alternative remedy thus lies at the heart of the court's assessment in every such case.\ue200\ue202turn6search0\ue202turn0search1\ue202turn1search2\ue202turn10search4\ue202turn10search0\ue201

---

The applicable principles and case law are subject to continuous judicial development. Parties facing this issue in litigation should seek the guidance of a qualified Attorney-at-Law.

## QUESTION 1: WHETHER MUTUAL FUND REGULATIONS REQUIRE THE TRUSTEE TO VERIFY THAT REDEMPTION PROCEEDS ARE PAID TO THE ORIGINATING INVESTOR ACCOUNT

### 1.1 Division of Responsibilities Under the Trust Deed

At the outset, it is necessary to delineate clearly the respective duties of the Manager and the Trustee in respect of redemption payments, as prescribed by the Trust Deed.

**The Manager's Primary Role in Processing Redemptions:**

Under **Clause 14(A)(i)** of the Trust Deed, it is the **Manager** (Ceylon Asset Management Co. Ltd.) that bears the primary operational obligation in relation to redemptions. That Clause provides:

> *"the Manager shall on receipt by it or by its duly authorized agent of a request in writing by a Holder (other than the Manager) to purchase/redeem from such Holder...all or any part of the Units comprised in his holding at a price per Unit then in issue..."*

**Clause 14(B)(i)** further provides that no redemption request shall be valid unless the Holder delivers to the **Manager** in writing the notification of units to be redeemed. The Manager is therefore the gateway through which all redemption instructions flow.

Under **Clause 8(A)** of the Trust Deed, the register of unit holders is maintained by the **Manager** (not the Trustee), and **Clause 6** confirms that the Manager is the only entity recognised as having access to unit holder details.

**The Trustee's Role in Executing Payments:**

Under **Clause 22(A)**, any monies payable to a Holder "may be paid by electronic transfer or crossed cheque made payable to the **order of such Holder**." This provision implicitly requires that payment be directed to the account of the registered Holder — and only to that account — thereby tying the payment destination to the identity of the registered unit holder.

The Trustee's role in this process is that of executing payments out of the Deposited Property on the instructions of the Manager.

### 1.2 The Trustee's Duty of Due Diligence Under the Trust Deed

Notwithstanding the foregoing division of operational duties, the Trust Deed imposes a critical affirmative duty of care upon the Trustee. **Clause 24(B)(i)** provides that the Trustee shall:

> *"exercise due diligence and vigilance in carrying out its functions and duties and **in watching the rights and interests of Holders**"*

This is not a passive duty. The phrase "watching the rights and interests of Holders" imports an active supervisory obligation upon the Trustee — requiring the Trustee to monitor the operations of the Manager to ensure that the Holders' interests are protected. The Trustee cannot, in the discharge of this duty, be a mere mechanical executor of the Manager's instructions.

The significance of this duty is further underlined by **Clause 28(D)** of the Trust Deed, which expressly provides:

> *"nothing in any of the provisions of these presents shall in any case in which the Trustee and/or the Manager, as the case may be, have failed to show the degree of diligence and care required of them by the provisions of these presents exempt them from or indemnify them against any liability for breach of trust or any liability which by virtue of any rule of law would otherwise attach to them in respect of any negligence, default, breach of duty of trust of which they may be guilty in relation to their duties."*

Accordingly, the protection afforded to the Trustee under **Clause 30(B)** — which permits the Trustee to rely on instructions received from the Manager — **does not** extinguish the Trustee's independent duty of due diligence. The Trustee remains liable for negligence in the execution of Manager instructions, including negligence in failing to verify the correctness of payment destinations.

### 1.3 The Mandatory Obligation Under the Central Bank of Sri Lanka Gazette Notification

This is, in our opinion, the **most critical** regulatory provision bearing directly on the question posed.

The Central Bank of Sri Lanka, acting under **Sections 7, 10, 11 and 15 of the Exchange Control Act (Chapter 423)**, issued the **Gazette Notification on Unit Trusts** (Gazette No. 1719/22, dated 18th August 2011), granting permission for foreign investors to subscribe to units in Sri Lankan Unit Trusts subject to specific conditions. \ue202turn0search1

**Clause 2(c)** of that Gazette Notification provides as follows:

> *"A managing company, **trustee**, licensed commercial bank, a licensed broker or **any other person entrusted with the payment of monies such as redemption proceeds, sale proceeds, monies or commissions** in respect of any transaction permitted hereunder **shall make such payments only into or out of a Securities Investment Account** referred to in sub-paragraph (b) above."* \ue202turn0search1

**Clause 2(b)** defines the Securities Investment Account ("**SIA**") as a specific account opened by the investor at a licensed commercial bank in Sri Lanka in accordance with the directions of the Controller of Exchange, through which all purchase payments are to be channelled. \ue202turn0search1

The combined effect of Clauses 2(b) and 2(c) is therefore as follows:
- All investments (subscriptions) into a Unit Trust by foreign investors must be made through a designated SIA.
- All redemption proceeds must be paid **only** into the same SIA — that is, the originating account through which the investment was made.
- This obligation is imposed **explicitly upon the Trustee** by name.

This provision constitutes a **mandatory regulatory obligation** imposed by the Controller of Exchange acting under the Exchange Control Act, and it directly places a duty upon the Trustee to verify that redemption payments are directed to the correct Securities Investment Account, i.e., the originating investor account.

**Clause 38** and **Clause 45** of the Trust Deed further reinforce this position, as they expressly incorporate all applicable laws, regulations, and codes (including the CBSL Gazette Notification) as part of the Trust Deed itself.

### 1.4 Obligations Under the Collective Investment Scheme Code 2022

The **Collective Investment Scheme Code 2022** ("CIS Code"), which repealed and replaced the Unit Trust Code 2011, \ue200\ue202turn2search1\ue202turn2search2\ue201 imposes comprehensive governance obligations on trustees of collective investment schemes in Sri Lanka.

**Rule 19(2)(b)** of the CIS Code requires the Trustee to submit a half-yearly Compliance Report to the SEC confirming, inter alia, compliance with the **Rules and Regulations of the FIU of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka, including Rules and Regulations pertaining to Anti-Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing.** \ue202turn2search3

This obligation is reinforced by the **SEC Directive dated 4th October 2024** (Ref: SEC/SUP/2024/10/477), which directs all Trustees of Collective Investment Schemes to submit compliance reports in the prescribed format, confirming adherence to all applicable regulatory requirements including AML/CFT rules. \ue202turn2search3

The Trustee **cannot** certify compliance with AML/CFT requirements in its Compliance Report to the SEC without having implemented adequate internal controls to verify the destination of redemption payments. The obligation to file such a Compliance Report therefore necessarily implies that the Trustee must maintain appropriate verification mechanisms.

### 1.5 Obligations Under the AML/CFT Framework

Deutsche Bank AG, Colombo Branch, as a **Licensed Commercial Bank** in Sri Lanka, independently carries the following AML/CFT obligations entirely separate from its role as Trustee:

**(a)** Under the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act No. 5 of 2006** ("PMLA"), \ue202turn7search0 the processing of funds to incorrect or unverified accounts without adequate checks could constitute facilitation of money laundering and expose the Bank to criminal liability.

**(b)** Under the **Financial Transactions Reporting Act No. 6 of 2006** ("FTRA"), all licensed commercial banks are required reporting entities obligated to conduct Customer Due Diligence ("CDD") on all transactions, including international wire transfers. \ue202turn7search1

**(c)** The **FIU of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka** has issued guidelines and circulars requiring financial institutions to implement enhanced due diligence ("EDD") for high-risk transactions, including cross-border wire transfers. \ue202turn3search0

The Fund's unique status as **the only USD-denominated Unit Trust in Sri Lanka** with Central Bank approval to remit redemption proceeds to any country in the world places this Fund in an elevated risk category under AML/CFT standards. The cross-border nature of redemption payments, involving foreign beneficiaries and international wire transfers to various jurisdictions, heightens the due diligence obligations of Deutsche Bank in its dual capacity as Trustee and as the executing bank for these payments.

Processing a wire transfer to an incorrect country — even inadvertently — raises AML/CFT red flags and could implicate the Bank in respect of suspicious transaction reporting obligations under the FTRA and PMLA.

### 1.6 Our Considered Opinion

Drawing together the foregoing analysis, we are of the considered opinion that:

**(i)** The **CBSL Gazette Notification** issued under the Exchange Control Act constitutes a **direct and mandatory regulatory obligation** on the Trustee to ensure that redemption proceeds are paid only into the originating Securities Investment Account of the investor. This is not merely a best-practice recommendation — it is a binding legal requirement;

**(ii)** The Trustee's duty of **due diligence and vigilance** under **Clause 24(B)(i)** of the Trust Deed, read together with **Clause 28(D)**, independently requires the Trustee to implement adequate verification controls for redemption payments, and the Trustee cannot evade liability for negligence by relying solely on Manager instructions;

**(iii)** The **CIS Code 2022** and the **SEC Directive of October 2024** collectively require the Trustee to maintain internal controls sufficient to certify compliance with AML/CFT requirements — which necessarily requires account verification mechanisms;

**(iv)** Deutsche Bank's independent obligations as a **licensed commercial bank** under the **PMLA** and **FTRA** require it to conduct due diligence on the destination of international wire transfers, irrespective of its role as Trustee;

**(v)** While the **primary operational responsibility** for processing redemption requests and directing payment instructions rests with the **Manager** under the Trust Deed, the Trustee's independent verification obligation is a separate and additional layer of control that cannot be abrogated by the Trustee's reliance on Manager instructions alone.

In sum, the answer to Question 1 is **in the affirmative**: mutual fund regulations in Sri Lanka — specifically the CBSL Gazette Notification, the CIS Code 2022, and the applicable AML/CFT framework — collectively require the Trustee to implement an independent verification mechanism to ensure that redemption proceeds are paid to the originating investor account.

**2. THE REGULATORY POSITION: DOES THE TRUSTEE BEAR AN OBLIGATION TO VERIFY THAT REDEMPTION PROCEEDS ARE REMITTED TO THE ORIGINATING INVESTOR ACCOUNT?**

We have given careful consideration to the regulatory framework governing this matter. We confirm our considered position as follows.

**2.1 The Trust Deed**

The Unit Trust Deed dated 2nd July 2014 constituting the Fund (the "**Trust Deed**") divides the operational responsibilities between the Manager and the Trustee in relation to redemptions. Under **Clause 14(A)(i)**, the primary obligation to receive and process redemption requests rests with the **Manager**. The Manager receives the redemption request from the unit holder, verifies it, and instructs the Trustee to execute the payment.

The Trustee is, in the ordinary course, entitled to rely upon the instructions of the Manager (Clause 30(B) of the Trust Deed). However, this reliance does not relieve the Trustee of its independent duty of **"due diligence and vigilance in carrying out its functions and duties and in watching the rights and interests of Holders"** under **Clause 24(B)(i)**. Furthermore, **Clause 28(D)** expressly provides that the Trustee shall not be exonerated from liability for **negligence** in the discharge of its duties, notwithstanding any good faith reliance on Manager instructions. **Clause 22(A)** further provides that payments made to unit holders must be made to "the order of such Holder" — confirming that payments must be directed to the registered unit holder's account.

Finally, **Clause 38** and **Clause 45** of the Trust Deed expressly incorporate all applicable laws, regulations, and codes (including those referred to below) as integral components of the Trust Deed.

**2.2 Central Bank of Sri Lanka Gazette Notification**

The most directly applicable regulatory provision on the matter at hand is the **Draft Gazette Notification on Unit Trusts** issued by the Central Bank of Sri Lanka under the Exchange Control Act (Gazette No. 1719/22, dated 18th August 2011), under which foreign investors were granted permission to invest in Sri Lankan Unit Trusts, subject to mandatory conditions. The relevant condition at **Clause 2(c)** of that Gazette Notification provides as follows:

> *"A managing company, **trustee**, licensed commercial bank, a licensed broker or any other person entrusted with the payment of monies such as redemption proceeds, sale proceeds, monies or commissions in respect of any transaction permitted hereunder **shall make such payments only into or out of a Securities Investment Account**..."*

The **Securities Investment Account** ("**SIA**"), as defined in the said Gazette Notification, is the specific account designated by the investor at a licensed commercial bank in Sri Lanka through which the original investment in the Fund is channelled. The effect of this provision is unambiguous: **redemption proceeds must be remitted only to the originating Securities Investment Account**, and this obligation is imposed **directly and by name upon the Trustee**.

This constitutes a **mandatory and binding legal obligation** on Deutsche Bank as Trustee, imposed by the Controller of Exchange acting under the Exchange Control Act. We are accordingly of the view that an independent verification check by the Trustee — to ensure that redemption proceeds are remitted to the correct originating account — is not merely a matter of good practice but is a **regulatory requirement** under Sri Lankan law.

**2.3 The Collective Investment Scheme Code 2022 and the SEC Directive of October 2024**

The **Collective Investment Scheme Code 2022** ("**CIS Code**"), which came into force on 15th March 2022 and repealed the Unit Trust Code 2011, prescribes comprehensive obligations on Trustees of Collective Investment Schemes. Under **Rule 19(2)(b)** of the CIS Code, the Trustee is required to submit a half-yearly Compliance Report to the Securities and Exchange Commission of Sri Lanka ("**SEC**"), confirming inter alia compliance with the Rules and Regulations of the **Financial Intelligence Unit** ("**FIU**") of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka pertaining to **Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing** ("**AML/CFT**"). This obligation is reinforced by the **SEC Directive dated 4th October 2024** (Ref: SEC/SUP/2024/10/477), which renders such compliance reporting mandatory for all Trustees of Collective Investment Schemes.

The Trustee plainly cannot certify compliance with AML/CFT requirements in its Compliance Report to the SEC without maintaining adequate internal controls to verify the destination of redemption payments. The obligation to file such Compliance Reports therefore necessarily implies that the Trustee must implement and maintain a verification mechanism in respect of redemption payment destinations.

**2.4 AML/CFT Framework**

You have correctly identified the AML/CFT dimension of this matter. We confirm that, as a **Licensed Commercial Bank** in Sri Lanka, Deutsche Bank AG, Colombo Branch, independently carries AML/CFT obligations under the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act No. 5 of 2006** and the **Financial Transactions Reporting Act No. 6 of 2006**, which require it to conduct Customer Due Diligence on all significant transactions and in particular on international wire transfers.

As you rightly note, the Fund carries a unique and elevated risk profile. It is the **only USD-denominated Unit Trust in Sri Lanka** with Central Bank approval to remit redemption proceeds to any country in the world. This cross-border character — with redemption proceeds potentially being remitted to foreign bank accounts across multiple jurisdictions — places the Fund's transactions in a category warranting enhanced due diligence under AML/CFT standards. Processing redemption payments to unverified or incorrect overseas accounts, even inadvertently, creates a serious risk of facilitating money laundering or financial fraud, and could expose both CAM and Deutsche Bank to significant regulatory and criminal liability under the aforesaid legislation.

**2.5 Summary of Regulatory Position**

Having regard to the foregoing, we confirm that the **regulatory position is clear**: the Trustee is under a **direct mandatory obligation** under the CBSL Gazette Notification to ensure that redemption proceeds are paid only to the originating Securities Investment Account of the investor. This is reinforced by the Trustee's duty of due diligence under the Trust Deed, the compliance certification requirements of the CIS Code 2022, and Deutsche Bank's independent AML/CFT obligations as a licensed commercial bank.

We wish to emphasise that the **primary operational responsibility** for receiving and processing redemption instructions remains with the **Manager**. However, the Trustee's regulatory obligation to verify the destination of redemption payments constitutes a **separate, independent, and non-delegable layer of control** that cannot be discharged merely by reliance on instructions received from the Manager.

**3. PROPOSED REMEDIAL MEASURES AND ENHANCED INTERNAL CONTROLS**

With immediate effect, Deutsche Bank Colombo Branch shall implement a mandatory verification protocol as part of its internal processing of all redemption payments for the Fund. Under this protocol, the processing officer shall, prior to executing any redemption payment, cross-verify the beneficiary bank details — including the bank name, SWIFT code, account number, and **country** — against:

- the investor's KYC documentation on file; and
- the verified list of originating investor accounts to be shared with us by CAM on a weekly basis.

Any discrepancy between the payment instruction received from CAM and the verified originating account details shall be immediately escalated to designated Senior Officers of both CAM and Deutsche Bank for resolution prior to processing. **(b) Host-to-Host (H2H) Payment File Transfer**

We agree in principle to the implementation of a Host-to-Host file transfer mechanism for redemption payment instructions between CAM's MSL system and Deutsche Bank's payment processing system, akin to the existing system-to-system arrangement in place with Hatton National Bank for the other seven funds under your management. We will engage our internal technology team and request your team to engage the MSL System Vendors to assess the feasibility of implementation and to agree on an implementation timeline at the earliest opportunity.

**(c) Three-Tier Authorization for Redemption Payments**

We agree to the implementation of a **three-tier authorisation framework** within our payment processing system for all redemption payments of the Fund, structured as follows:

- **Tier 1 — Operations Officer:** Responsible for data input, SWIFT code verification, and initial confirmation of beneficiary account details against the verified originating account list;
- **Tier 2 — Compliance Officer:** Responsible for AML/CFT review, confirming that the beneficiary account matches the investor's KYC profile and the originating account details; and
- **Tier 3 — Senior Manager:** Responsible for final review and authorisation of the payment, with explicit sign-off confirming that all verifications have been completed satisfactorily.

The following observations, offered by way of supplementary guidance, are for the exclusive use of Deutsche Bank and are not for inclusion in the response email to CAM:

### 3.1 Internal Accountability

The Incident Report furnished by CAM clearly identifies specific named officers of Deutsche Bank as having been involved in the erroneous input and authorisation of the payment:

- **Nuwanga** — provided first-tier authorisation without detecting the error; and

### 3.2 Trustee Liability

As noted above, **Clause 28(D)** of the Trust Deed expressly preserves the Trustee's liability for negligence. The processing of a payment to an incorrect country — an error that is, in principle, readily detectable by any reasonably diligent officer familiar with international SWIFT codes — could potentially constitute negligence on the part of the Trustee. Deutsche Bank is advised to assess its potential exposure in this regard, and to ensure that adequate professional indemnity and operational risk insurance coverage is maintained.

### 3.3 Regulatory Risk

Given the mandatory regulatory obligation imposed by the CBSL Gazette Notification to ensure that redemption payments are channelled only through the originating Securities Investment Account, the Leitch Redemption Incident may constitute a technical breach of that Gazette Notification. Deutsche Bank is advised to take legal advice on whether a self-disclosure to the Controller of Exchange (Department of Foreign Exchange) is warranted, in addition to the disclosure to the SEC recommended above.

### 3.4 Urgency

The implementation of the Pre-Approved Beneficiary Account Registry and the enhanced three-tier authorisation protocol should be treated as a **matter of the utmost urgency**. Deutsche Bank is advised not to process any further redemption payments for the Fund until these controls are at least partially in place, given the elevated regulatory and reputational risk associated with a recurrence.

Under the **PMLA**, the processing of funds to incorrect, unverified, or illegitimate overseas accounts — even if done inadvertently — creates a risk of facilitating money laundering and could expose Deutsche Bank to serious criminal and regulatory liability. \ue202turn3search2 The Bank's obligations under the PMLA require it to take all reasonable steps to ensure that financial transactions are legitimate and that the destination of funds corresponds to the verified identity of the beneficial owner.

**6.2 Financial Transactions Reporting Act No. 6 of 2006**

Under the **FTRA**, Deutsche Bank, as a reporting institution, is obligated to conduct ongoing Customer Due Diligence ("**CDD**") and transaction monitoring. International wire transfers — particularly those involving remittances to overseas accounts in potentially any jurisdiction in the world — are precisely the category of high-risk transactions requiring enhanced due diligence ("**EDD**") under the FTRA and the FIU Guidelines. The FIU of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka has consistently emphasised the importance of proper transaction monitoring for cross-border remittances. \ue202turn3search1

**6.3 The Elevated Risk Profile of the CBDF**

The CBDF carries a risk profile that is, by its own nature, elevated. This cross-border character — involving international wire transfers to foreign bank accounts across potentially any jurisdiction — places the Fund's redemption transactions in a category that warrants, as a matter of regulatory obligation rather than merely good practice, the highest level of due diligence and beneficiary account verification. A failure to implement adequate verification controls in this context is not merely a procedural lapse; it is a failure of AML/CFT compliance.
